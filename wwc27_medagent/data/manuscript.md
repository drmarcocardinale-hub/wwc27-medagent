%TITLE Sports Medicine and Science Considerations to Maximise Preparation for the FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027: From Static Evidence to a Living Agent
%RUNNING Preparing for the FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027

%KEYPOINTS
Men's and women's World Cups show that in-tournament injury and illness rates are low but still matter. Respiratory infection is the most common time-loss illness. Female-specific health domains remain under-recorded.
Brazil 2027 will present very different conditions from venue to venue: a cool, wet south, a warm and humid north-east, a dry upland centre, domestic flights of up to about 3200 km, and circulating arboviral disease. Screening and monitoring should therefore be planned venue by venue.
We propose publishing tournament medical guidance as a validated, queryable "paper agent" as well as a static article. A human curator keeps the agent current, and every answer is traceable to its source.
%ENDKEYPOINTS

%ABSTRACT
The FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027 (24 June–25 July; 32 teams; eight host cities) will be played in the southern-hemisphere winter across a country the size of a continent. Medical teams can draw on two decades of injury and illness surveillance at men's World Cups, including the Brazil 2014 and North America 2026 tournaments; a smaller literature from the 2019 and 2023 Women's World Cups; and recent consensus work on female athlete health. This Current Opinion combines these sources into a screening and monitoring framework for Brazil 2027. The framework is organised by phase (pre-tournament, preparation camp, in-tournament, post-tournament) and by ten health domains, from cardiovascular and musculoskeletal screening to energy availability, menstrual health, heat, infection, mental health, concussion, travel and medication. Host-city climate varies more than the image of a tropical tournament suggests. Indicative wet-bulb globe temperatures range from below 20 °C in Porto Alegre to about 30 °C or more in Fortaleza and Recife, and infection control and vaccination deserve as much planning as heat. Because this evidence will keep changing up to the tournament, we also propose a new way to publish it. Building on the Paper2Agent framework, we present a prototype "paper agent": a Model Context Protocol server that lets practitioners query the article's tables, decision tools and evidence base in natural language. Its outputs are tested against the source data and a 50-item validation benchmark, and a named human curator keeps it up to date. We discuss governance and the limits of AI-supported guidance.
%ENDABSTRACT

## 1 Introduction

Team physicians preparing for a World Cup must turn evidence scattered across many sources into decisions about individual players, often months ahead and with incomplete information. The FIFA Women's World Cup (FWWC) Brazil 2027 makes this harder than usual. The tournament runs from 24 June to 25 July 2027, with 64 matches in eight host cities stretching from Porto Alegre in the far south to Fortaleza, just below the equator. Group-stage matches finish on 8 July, and the final is at the Maracanã in Rio de Janeiro [@FIFA2027]. Teams will travel across climate zones in the southern-hemisphere winter, a season many European-based players have rarely competed in.

The evidence base is uneven. FIFA has recorded injuries at its tournaments since 1998, and men's World Cups have produced a long series of epidemiological, environmental and medical-services data [@Junge2015; @Serner2025; @Schumacher2024]. Standardised surveillance of the women's tournament is much more recent. A report covering injuries, illnesses and mental-health problems with current consensus methods has been published only for Australia and New Zealand 2023 [@Lu2026]. Consensus statements on female athlete health have also been published only recently [@Moore2023; @Crossley2025]. Practitioners must therefore combine lessons from men's tournaments, which they cannot simply transfer to women, with a thinner women-specific literature and with host-country information that changes month to month (for example, arboviral outbreaks).

This article has two aims. First, we combine the lessons of previous men's and women's World Cups into a screening and monitoring framework for Brazil 2027. Second, we argue that guidance of this kind should not end as a static PDF. Recent work has shown that a research paper can be turned into an interactive, tested artificial-intelligence (AI) agent that carries out the paper's methods on request [@Miao2026]. We describe how we applied this idea to tournament medicine and present a prototype that practitioners can query while they prepare.

## 2 Lessons from Previous World Cups

### 2.1 Men's Tournaments: Declining Injury Rates, Persistent Respiratory Illness and a Growing Focus on Heat

At the 2014 FIFA World Cup in Brazil, team physicians reported 104 match injuries (1.68 per match), of which 0.97 per match were expected to cause time loss. Injury incidence at World Cups fell by 37% between 2002 and 2014 [@Junge2015]. Brazil 2014 is also the most relevant precedent for environmental risk. Of the 64 matches, 16 were played under high environmental stress based on wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and humidity. Under those conditions players covered less distance at high intensity (24.8 vs 26.9 m·min⁻¹) and made about 10% fewer sprints, while total distance, playing time and goals were preserved. Players appeared to adjust their pacing rather than lose output overall [@Nassis2015]. A retrospective climatological analysis found that only two matches in 2014 were played above 30 °C [@Lucena2017]. This is a reminder that the image of a tropical tournament can overstate the risk in some venues and understate it in others.

At Qatar 2022 (705 of 838 players), the time-loss injury incidence was 5.6 per 1000 h. Match injury incidence (20.6 per 1000 h) was the lowest recorded at a World Cup, and hamstring strain was the most common diagnosis. Twelve of the 15 time-loss illnesses (80%) were respiratory infections [@Serner2025]. The Qatar polyclinic data also show what teams actually needed. Thirty of the 32 teams used central medical services at least once, and imaging was the service requested most often. Seventeen teams asked for extra equipment or clinical help [@Schumacher2024]. For 2026, host cities spanned about 4300 km, and extreme heat was expected in 14 of 16 cities (historical maximum WBGT 21–35 °C) [@Esh2026]. FIFA introduced mandatory 3-min hydration breaks at the 22nd minute of each half in every match, whatever the conditions [@FIFA2025]. We expect peer-reviewed surveillance data from 2026 soon. These data should be added to the resource described in Sect. 5 as soon as they are published.

### 2.2 Women's Tournaments: Low Burden, Different Patterns

At FWWC 2023, 26 of 32 teams (536 of 742 players) reported data using the football-specific consensus methods [@Walden2023]. The time-loss injury incidence was 5.2 per 1000 h, 14.0 per 1000 h in matches and 2.2 per 1000 h in training. The knee and thigh were injured most often, the lower leg carried the highest burden, and 59% of time-loss injuries involved contact. Respiratory illness was again the most frequent illness category. The incidence of mental-health problems was low (0.2 per 1000 player-days), and overall injury incidence and burden were lower than at earlier tournaments of either sex [@Lu2026]. Video analysis comparing the 2019 Women's and 2018 Men's World Cups found more injury stoppages in women's matches (81.2 vs 56.8 per 1000 match-hours). Women were less often substituted after an incident (15.3% vs 27.3%) [@Donmez2025]. On-field medical teams should plan for frequent assessments and should not read time-loss rates alone as a measure of workload.

Several FWWC studies are directly useful for preparation. National-team menstrual-cycle tracking at FWWC 2019 found higher session ratings of perceived exertion in the luteal phase for comparable running volumes. Fatigue 48 h after matches was also greater in the luteal phase than in the follicular phase [@Scott2024]. This fits with club data showing higher muscle and non-contact injury rates in the luteal phase [@Barlow2024]. Team physicians at FWWC 2019 relied mainly on subjective wellness, heart rate, playing minutes and daily medical screening. They named time limits, schedule uncertainty and inconsistent club recommendations as barriers [@Saltzman2023]. Among players, 15% had no permanent medical staff at their domestic club [@Geertsema2021]. For these players, national-team screening may be the most complete health assessment they receive all year. At FWWC 2023, 55% of medical staff reported feeling pressured by players, and 47% by coaching staff, when assessing a suspected concussion [@Wilke2024]. Match demands are high and vary widely between teams. Teams covered 103 km per match on average in 2023, and the teams with the most high-intensity running covered 24–44% more at high intensity than the teams with the least [@Bradley2025]. Finally, mean medication use per player was higher at the Women's than the Men's World Cup (0.85 vs 0.77 substances per player per match), with similar non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use [@Tscholl2015]. Medication monitoring therefore belongs in the plan.

### 2.3 What Is Still Missing

Three gaps limit how well men's evidence transfers to women's tournaments. First, women are under-represented in exercise thermoregulation research, making up 12–18% of participants between 2010 and 2019 [@Hutchins2021]. Evidence on cooling strategies specifically in women is sparse [@Convit2024], and core temperature is higher in the luteal phase at rest and after exercise [@Giersch2020]. Second, the ten female health domains proposed as a supplement to the International Olympic Committee (IOC) surveillance statement, including menstrual, breast and pelvic-floor health, are not yet routinely reported at tournaments [@Moore2023]. Third, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury has the greatest burden in elite women's club football (median 292 days lost) [@Hallen2024]. A 5-week tournament cannot measure ACL risk or show whether prevention is working, so pre-tournament screening and club–country data sharing matter more.

## 3 Brazil 2027: A Tournament of Contrasts

Table 1 summarises the host-city climate normals for June and July, the months of the tournament. The venues fall into three groups. Porto Alegre and São Paulo are cool; Porto Alegre's mean daily temperature is about 14 °C with frequent rain. Brasília and Belo Horizonte are dry and upland (1172 and 852 m) with mild afternoons, cool nights and relative humidity that falls to about 51% in Brasília in July. Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, Recife and Fortaleza are warm and humid coastal cities; Fortaleza averages daily maxima above 30 °C with about 77–80% humidity. Our indicative WBGT estimates, calculated from climate normals (Table 1), range from below 20 °C to about 35 °C across the tournament. The main planning problem is therefore variability rather than uniform heat. A team may need heat-mitigation measures for a group match in Fortaleza and cold-weather warm-up routines a few days later in Porto Alegre. The two cities are about 3200 km apart. For comparison, FIFA's historical policy made cooling breaks mandatory above a WBGT of 32 °C, and FIFPRO recommends cooling breaks from 28 °C [@FIFPRO]. These estimates cannot replace on-site WBGT measurement, which should follow IOC recommendations for events in the heat [@Racinais2022].

Infection deserves at least as much attention as heat. Brazil recorded its largest dengue epidemic in 2024, with about 6 million probable cases by June, and was the first country to add a tetravalent dengue vaccine to its public health system [@GurgelGoncalves2024]. Oropouche virus has re-emerged [@Scachetti2025]. Arboviral transmission peaks in the rainy season from November to May, so June–July carries lower but not negligible risk, especially in the humid north-east. Current United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidance recommends yellow fever vaccination for travellers to most host states, including the Federal District, Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Minas Gerais and Rio Grande do Sul. It does not recommend vaccination for Pernambuco or Ceará, and recommends it only for designated areas of Bahia [@CDC2026]. Vaccination takes time to arrange, so these decisions belong in the pre-tournament medical assessment, not the final week before travel. Respiratory infection, the leading illness at recent World Cups [@Serner2025; @Lu2026], will be spread by travel, crowded venues and dry air in Brasília. Hygiene, vaccination (including influenza and measles status) and isolation protocols are therefore core parts of the tournament medical plan.

## 4 A Screening and Monitoring Framework for Brazil 2027

We propose a framework organised by phase and health domain (Table 2). Three principles guide it. First, screening should be done once, well in advance, and reused. The pre-tournament assessment, ideally 6–12 months before the tournament, should include history, examination and a 12-lead electrocardiogram. It should use sex-appropriate interpretation and echocardiography where indicated. In 3121 elite English female players, screening identified major cardiac conditions in 0.2%, mostly electrical disorders, and no sudden cardiac arrest occurred during follow-up [@Yamagata2026]. This supports screening with appropriate budgeting and interpretation. The same assessment should include ACL and hamstring risk profiles and adherence to neuromuscular training. Such programmes, at a minimum dose of about 10 min twice weekly, reduce ACL injuries by about 61% in female athletes (high-certainty evidence) [@Bullock2025; @Crossley2025]. It should also cover energy availability and iron status, using the IOC Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) tools [@Mountjoy2023; @SmithRyan2026], and a mental-health screen with validated instruments [@Reardon2026].

Second, monitoring during the tournament should be lean and consistent. Staff should record a small set of daily measures: wellness, sleep, illness symptoms, menstrual-cycle phase and symptoms (with consent), body mass change and urine colour on hot days, and playing minutes. Records should follow the football-specific surveillance consensus and its female health supplement, so that data can be pooled and compared [@Walden2023; @Moore2023]. Experience from Qatar shows that centralised oversight, consistent staffing and electronic tools improve data quality [@Tabben2025]. Because exercise raises hepcidin 1.5–2.5-fold for several hours [@Fensham2023], iron tests during the tournament should be timed carefully, and supplementation should be planned before the tournament rather than started reactively.

Third, environmental plans should be specific to each venue and to each player. Players whose group matches are in the north-east should heat-acclimate for 1–2 weeks beforehand [@Racinais2015; @Esh2026]. On hot days, individual cooling should be planned (ice slurry or ice vests, which have the best, if limited, evidence in women) [@Convit2024]. Staff should allow for higher luteal-phase core temperature [@Giersch2020]. Recovery and exposure should be adjusted for cold, wet conditions in the south. Travel should be managed with evidence-based jet-lag and travel-fatigue strategies [@JanseVanRensburg2021]. European teams will cross 4–5 time zones on arrival and teams from Asia and Oceania many more. All host cities share one time zone, but domestic flights can be long. Food and water hygiene and mosquito-bite prevention complete the plan.

## 5 From Paper to Practitioner Agent: A Living, AI-Supported Resource

Guidance like Table 2 goes out of date quickly. New surveillance papers, outbreak reports, confirmed kick-off times and base-camp decisions will all appear between now and June 2027. Living systematic reviews and living guidelines address this by updating individual recommendations as evidence arrives [@Elliott2017; @Akl2017]. However, practitioners still receive the result as static text that they must read, interpret and apply to their own squad.

Paper2Agent offers a different model. It automatically converts a paper and its code into a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server with three parts: tools (tested functions that carry out the paper's methods), resources (the manuscript, data and supplementary material) and prompts (step-by-step instructions for common workflows). Any compatible AI assistant can then use the server as a "virtual corresponding author" [@Miao2026]. The generated tools are checked against the paper's own outputs before release. In a 100-paper evaluation, 74 papers were successfully converted and 593 of 599 proposed tools passed automated validation. The agents answered tutorial-based questions more accurately than a general AI coding assistant given the code repository (91.2% vs 80.3–86.3%), and they rejected all out-of-scope questions in an adversarial test [@Miao2026].

We applied this model to the present article (Fig. 1). The prototype, WWC27-MedAgent, exposes the following:

- Tools: a host-city environmental profile and indicative heat-risk estimate; a travel-burden calculator between venues; a phase- and domain-specific screening checklist generator; and an evidence lookup that returns only sources with digital object identifiers (DOIs).
- Resources: this manuscript, Tables 1–2 in machine-readable form, and a versioned evidence table listing each claim, its numeric value and its source.
- Prompts: a "pre-tournament medical plan" workflow that asks for the team's base camp, fixtures and squad profile, calls the tools in order and produces a draft plan for clinician review.

In version 0.1, 41 automated tests check the tools' outputs against the source values reported here, confirm that every claim resolves to a DOI or URL, and confirm that the agent declines questions outside its evidence base rather than inventing an answer. The agent is updated in "living" mode: a named curator adds new evidence (for example, 2026 World Cup surveillance data, outbreak bulletins and final kick-off times), re-runs the tests and releases a new dated version with a change log. The code, data and benchmark are openly available on GitHub, and each release is archived on Zenodo with its own DOI; a concept DOI always resolves to the latest version. As an example, we asked the agent to plan for a hypothetical team based in São Paulo with group matches in Fortaleza, Recife and Porto Alegre. It returned legs of about 2355, 610 and 2976 km (5941 km in total). It marked Fortaleza and Recife as heat-exposed venues (indicative sWBGT at the daily maximum above 32 °C) and Porto Alegre as a cool venue needing cold- and wet-weather routines. It reported that yellow fever vaccination is recommended for Rio Grande do Sul but not for Ceará or Pernambuco. It then assembled the matching phase-specific checklist, with every item linked to its source. Individual player data never pass through the public server. Teams that want personalised outputs can run the open-source server locally, inside their own data-governance environment.

To test whether the agent works, and not only whether its tools compute correctly, we built a 50-item validation benchmark modelled on the Paper2Agent evaluation [@Miao2026] (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] 1). Twenty reproduction questions ask for values reported in this article. Ten application questions need the tools to be used in new ways, such as the total flying distance for an itinerary or the heat band for a match-day forecast. Ten open-ended scenarios, such as low ferritin six weeks before the tournament, are scored by two blinded raters against a source-linked rubric. Ten out-of-scope questions, including near-misses such as injury rates at the 2015 tournament, should be declined. Ground truths are computed from the versioned data, so the benchmark is updated with each release. Each item is put five times to the agent and to two baselines (the same model with the full article in its prompt, and with neither article nor tools). Accuracy is reported as mean ± standard error and rater agreement as Cohen's κ. At the tool layer, all 30 automatically scored ground truths are reachable. In a held-out check, keyword retrieval returned the expected evidence for 7 of 10 in-scope questions and declined 7 of 10 out-of-scope questions, so the language model must handle synonyms and near-misses. End-to-end results will be archived with the corresponding release.

This approach has real limits. Large language models can extract football injury data accurately once carefully prompted, but performance improves only after refinement [@LopezAlvarez2026]. Answers to open clinical questions can be defensible without being correct. Paper2Agent's authors also emphasise that researchers, and in our setting clinicians, remain responsible for evaluating outputs [@Miao2026]. We therefore present the agent as a structured way to find, apply and audit the evidence in this article, not as a source of clinical decisions.

## 6 Limitations and Future Directions

This is an opinion piece based on published data. Surveillance from women's tournaments comes mainly from one edition, and climate normals describe typical rather than extreme conditions. Venue microclimates, kick-off times and heat waves may differ substantially from Table 1. The retrieval layer misses some synonyms, and the end-to-end benchmark had not been run at submission. Its results, together with additional questions written by independent team physicians, are needed before the agent is recommended for routine use. We invite FIFA, confederations and teams to contribute anonymised, consensus-compliant surveillance data from Brazil 2027 so that the resource can update itself for 2031.

## 7 Conclusions

Brazil 2027 is best understood as several tournaments in one country. Previous World Cups point to predictable priorities: respiratory infection, contact and muscle injuries, heat in some venues, and the specific health needs of female players that remain under-studied. Teams that screen early, monitor a small set of measures consistently and plan each venue separately will be best placed to protect player health and performance. Publishing this guidance as a tested, human-curated agent that practitioners can query offers a practical way to keep it current up to the tournament and beyond.
