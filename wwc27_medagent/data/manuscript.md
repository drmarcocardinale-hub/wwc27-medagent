%TITLE Sports Medicine and Science Considerations to Maximise Preparation for the FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027: From Static Evidence to a Living Agent
%RUNNING Preparing for the FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027

%KEYPOINTS
Men's and women's World Cups show that in-tournament injury and illness rates are low but still matter. Respiratory infection is the most common time-loss illness. Female-specific health domains remain under-recorded.
Brazil 2027 will present very different conditions from venue to venue: a cool, wet south, a warm and humid north-east, a dry upland centre, city air quality that peaks in winter in the south-east, domestic flights of up to about 3200 km, and circulating arboviral disease. Screening and monitoring should be planned venue by venue.
We propose publishing tournament medical guidance as a validated, queryable "paper agent" as well as a static article. A human curator keeps the agent current, and every answer is traceable to its source.
%ENDKEYPOINTS

%ABSTRACT
The FIFA Women's World Cup Brazil 2027 (24 June–25 July; 32 teams; eight host cities) will be played in the southern-hemisphere winter across a country the size of a continent. Medical teams can draw on two decades of injury and illness surveillance at men's World Cups, including the Brazil 2014 and North America 2026 tournaments; a smaller literature from the 2019 and 2023 Women's World Cups; and recent consensus work on female athlete health. This Current Opinion combines these sources into a screening and monitoring framework for Brazil 2027. The framework is organised by phase (pre-tournament, preparation camp, in-tournament, post-tournament) and by ten health domains, from cardiovascular and musculoskeletal screening to energy availability, menstrual health, heat, infection, mental health, concussion, travel and medication. Host-city conditions vary more than the image of a tropical tournament suggests. Indicative wet-bulb globe temperatures range from below 20 °C in Porto Alegre to about 30 °C or more in Fortaleza and Recife; air quality, infection control and vaccination deserve as much planning as heat. Because this evidence will keep changing up to the tournament, we also propose a new way to publish it. Building on the Paper2Agent framework, we present a prototype "paper agent": a Model Context Protocol server that lets practitioners query the article's tables, decision tools and evidence base in natural language. Its outputs are tested against the source data and a 50-item validation benchmark, and a named human curator keeps it up to date. We discuss governance and the limits of AI guidance.
%ENDABSTRACT

## 1 Introduction

Team physicians preparing for a World Cup must turn evidence scattered across many sources into decisions about individual players, months ahead and with incomplete information. The FIFA Women's World Cup (FWWC) Brazil 2027 makes this harder than usual. It runs from 24 June to 25 July 2027, with 64 matches in eight host cities from Porto Alegre in the far south to Fortaleza, just below the equator; the group stage ends on 8 July and the final is at the Maracanã [@FIFA2027]. Teams will cross climate zones in the southern-hemisphere winter, a season many European-based players have rarely competed in.

The evidence base is uneven. FIFA has recorded injuries at its tournaments since 1998, and men's World Cups have produced a long series of epidemiological, environmental and medical-services data [@Junge2015; @Serner2025; @Schumacher2024]. Standardised surveillance of the women's tournament is much more recent. A report covering injuries, illnesses and mental-health problems with current consensus methods has been published only for Australia and New Zealand 2023 [@Lu2026]. Consensus statements on female athlete health have also been published only recently [@Moore2023; @Crossley2025]. Practitioners must therefore combine lessons from men's tournaments, which they cannot simply transfer to women, with a thinner women-specific literature and with host-country information that changes month to month (for example, arboviral outbreaks).

This article has two aims. First, we combine the lessons of previous men's and women's World Cups into a screening and monitoring framework for Brazil 2027. Second, we argue that such guidance should not end as a static PDF: recent work shows that a paper can become an interactive, tested artificial-intelligence (AI) agent that carries out its methods on request [@Miao2026]. We describe how we applied that idea to tournament medicine and present a prototype practitioners can query while they prepare.

## 2 Lessons from Previous World Cups

### 2.1 Men's Tournaments: Declining Injury Rates, Persistent Respiratory Illness and a Growing Focus on Heat

At the 2014 FIFA World Cup in Brazil, team physicians reported 104 match injuries (1.68 per match), of which 0.97 per match were expected to cause time loss. Injury incidence at World Cups fell by 37% between 2002 and 2014 [@Junge2015]. Brazil 2014 is also the most relevant precedent for environmental risk. Of the 64 matches, 16 were played under high environmental stress (wet-bulb globe temperature, WBGT, and humidity); under those conditions players covered less distance at high intensity (24.8 vs 26.9 m·min⁻¹) and made about 10% fewer sprints, while total distance, playing time and goals were preserved — pacing changed rather than output [@Nassis2015]. Only two matches in 2014 were played above 30 °C [@Lucena2017]: the image of a tropical tournament can overstate risk in some venues and understate it in others.

At Qatar 2022 (705 of 838 players), time-loss injury incidence was 5.6 per 1000 h; match incidence (20.6 per 1000 h) was the lowest recorded at a World Cup, hamstring strain the commonest diagnosis, and 12 of 15 time-loss illnesses (80%) were respiratory infections [@Serner2025]. Qatar's polyclinic data show what teams needed: 30 of 32 used central medical services at least once, imaging was the service requested most often, and 17 teams asked for extra equipment or clinical help [@Schumacher2024]. For 2026, host cities spanned about 4300 km, and extreme heat was expected in 14 of 16 cities (historical maximum WBGT 21–35 °C) [@Esh2026]. FIFA introduced mandatory 3-min hydration breaks at the 22nd minute of each half in every match, whatever the conditions [@FIFA2025]. Peer-reviewed surveillance from 2026 should be added to the resource described in Sect. 5 as soon as it appears.

### 2.2 Women's Tournaments: Low Burden, Different Patterns

At FWWC 2023, 26 of 32 teams (536 of 742 players) reported data using the football-specific consensus methods [@Walden2023]. Time-loss injury incidence was 5.2 per 1000 h (14.0 in matches, 2.2 in training); the knee and thigh were injured most often, the lower leg carried the highest burden, and 59% of time-loss injuries involved contact. Respiratory illness was again the most frequent illness, mental-health problems were rare (0.2 per 1000 player-days), and injury incidence and burden were lower than at earlier tournaments of either sex [@Lu2026]. Video analysis of the 2019 Women's and 2018 Men's World Cups found more injury stoppages in women's matches (81.2 vs 56.8 per 1000 match-hours) but fewer substitutions afterwards (15.3% vs 27.3%) [@Donmez2025]: plan for frequent on-field assessments, and do not read time-loss rates alone as workload.

Several FWWC studies are directly useful. Menstrual-cycle tracking at FWWC 2019 found higher session ratings of perceived exertion in the luteal phase for comparable running volumes, and greater fatigue 48 h after matches [@Scott2024]; club data show higher muscle and non-contact injury rates in the same phase [@Barlow2024]. Team physicians at FWWC 2019 relied mainly on subjective wellness, heart rate, playing minutes and daily screening, and named time, schedule uncertainty and inconsistent club recommendations as barriers [@Saltzman2023]. Among players, 15% had no permanent medical staff at their domestic club [@Geertsema2021]. For these players, national-team screening may be the most complete health assessment they receive all year. At FWWC 2023, 55% of medical staff reported feeling pressured by players, and 47% by coaching staff, when assessing a suspected concussion [@Wilke2024]. Match demands vary widely: teams covered 103 km per match on average in 2023, and the highest-ranked teams for high-intensity running covered 24–44% more of it than the lowest [@Bradley2025]. Finally, mean medication use per player was higher at the Women's than the Men's World Cup (0.85 vs 0.77 substances per player per match), with similar non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use [@Tscholl2015]. Medication monitoring therefore belongs in the plan.

### 2.3 What Is Still Missing

Three gaps limit how well men's evidence transfers. First, women made up only 12–18% of participants in exercise thermoregulation research between 2010 and 2019 [@Hutchins2021]; evidence on cooling strategies in women is sparse [@Convit2024], and core temperature is higher in the luteal phase at rest and after exercise [@Giersch2020]. Second, the ten female health domains proposed as a supplement to the International Olympic Committee (IOC) surveillance statement are not yet routinely reported at tournaments [@Moore2023]. Third, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury carries the greatest burden in elite women's club football (median 292 days lost) [@Hallen2024]; a 5-week tournament cannot measure that risk, so pre-tournament screening and club–country data sharing matter more.

## 3 Brazil 2027: A Tournament of Contrasts

Table 1 summarises the host-city climate normals for June and July, the months of the tournament. The venues fall into three groups. Porto Alegre and São Paulo are cool; Porto Alegre's mean daily temperature is about 14 °C with frequent rain. Brasília and Belo Horizonte are dry and upland (1172 and 852 m) with mild afternoons, cool nights and relative humidity that falls to about 51% in Brasília in July. Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, Recife and Fortaleza are warm and humid coastal cities; Fortaleza averages daily maxima above 30 °C with about 77–80% humidity. Our indicative WBGT estimates, calculated from climate normals (Table 1), range from below 20 °C to about 35 °C across the tournament. The main planning problem is therefore variability rather than uniform heat. A team may need heat-mitigation measures for a group match in Fortaleza and cold-weather warm-up routines a few days later in Porto Alegre. The two cities are about 3200 km apart. For comparison, FIFA's historical policy made cooling breaks mandatory above a WBGT of 32 °C, and FIFPRO recommends cooling breaks from 28 °C [@FIFPRO]. These estimates cannot replace on-site WBGT measurement, which should follow IOC recommendations for events in the heat [@Racinais2022].

Infection deserves at least as much attention as heat. Brazil recorded its largest dengue epidemic in 2024, with about 6 million probable cases by June, and was the first country to add a tetravalent dengue vaccine to its public health system [@GurgelGoncalves2024]. Oropouche virus has re-emerged [@Scachetti2025]. Arboviral transmission peaks in the rainy season from November to May, so June–July carries lower but not negligible risk, especially in the humid north-east. Current United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidance recommends yellow fever vaccination for travellers to most host states, including the Federal District, Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Minas Gerais and Rio Grande do Sul. It does not recommend vaccination for Pernambuco or Ceará, and recommends it only for designated areas of Bahia [@CDC2026]. Vaccination takes time to arrange, so these decisions belong in the pre-tournament medical assessment, not the final week before travel. Respiratory infection, the leading illness at recent World Cups [@Serner2025; @Lu2026], will be spread by travel, crowded venues and dry air in Brasília. Hygiene, vaccination (including influenza and measles status) and isolation protocols are therefore core parts of the tournament medical plan.

Air quality is the third environmental variable, and the one most often left out of tournament planning. São Paulo and Belo Horizonte have their most stagnant air in June and July, when traffic emissions accumulate under thermal inversions; Brasília combines very dry air with occasional regional biomass smoke; the north-eastern cities are well ventilated by trade winds; and Porto Alegre is cold and damp, itself an airway irritant. Pollution, aeroallergens, cold or dry air and altitude all modify exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) risk [@VanMeerbeke2024], and respiratory conditions affect roughly 20–70% of elite athletes depending on the sport [@Ora2024]. Because Brazil's national standards (CONAMA Resolution 506/2024) remain less strict than the 2021 WHO guideline levels [@WHO_AQG2021; @CONAMA2024], teams should judge conditions against the WHO levels and the state monitoring networks (CETESB in São Paulo, for example) rather than against compliance alone.

## 4 A Screening and Monitoring Framework for Brazil 2027

We propose a framework organised by phase and health domain (Table 2). Three principles guide it. First, screening should be done once, well in advance, and reused. The pre-tournament assessment, ideally 6–12 months before the tournament, should include history, examination and a 12-lead electrocardiogram. It should use sex-appropriate interpretation and echocardiography where indicated. In 3121 elite English female players, screening identified major cardiac conditions in 0.2%, mostly electrical disorders, and no sudden cardiac arrest occurred during follow-up [@Yamagata2026]. This supports screening with appropriate budgeting and interpretation. The same assessment should include ACL and hamstring risk profiles and adherence to neuromuscular training. Such programmes, at a minimum dose of about 10 min twice weekly, reduce ACL injuries by about 61% in female athletes (high-certainty evidence) [@Bullock2025; @Crossley2025]. It should also cover energy availability and iron status, using the IOC Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) tools [@Mountjoy2023; @SmithRyan2026], and a mental-health screen with validated instruments [@Reardon2026].

Second, in-tournament monitoring should be lean and consistent: a small set of daily measures (wellness, sleep, illness symptoms, cycle phase and symptoms with consent, body-mass change on hot days, playing minutes), recorded with the football-specific surveillance consensus and its female health supplement so that data can be pooled [@Walden2023; @Moore2023]. Qatar's experience shows that centralised oversight, consistent staffing and electronic tools improve data quality [@Tabben2025]. Because exercise raises hepcidin 1.5–2.5-fold for several hours [@Fensham2023], iron tests during the tournament should be timed carefully, and supplementation should be planned before the tournament rather than started reactively.

Third, environmental plans should be specific to each venue and player. Teams with north-eastern fixtures should heat-acclimate for 1–2 weeks [@Racinais2015; @Esh2026], plan individual cooling for hot days (ice slurry or ice vests, which have the best, if limited, evidence in women) [@Convit2024], allow for higher luteal-phase core temperature [@Giersch2020], adjust recovery for cold, wet southern venues, and manage travel with evidence-based jet-lag strategies [@JanseVanRensburg2021]. European teams will cross 4–5 time zones on arrival and teams from Asia and Oceania many more. All host cities share one time zone, but domestic flights can be long. Food and water hygiene and mosquito-bite prevention complete the plan. Airway health belongs in the same venue-specific logic: identify players with asthma or EIB before travel and confirm the diagnosis objectively, since resting spirometry is often normal [@He2022]; base treatment on inhaled corticosteroids rather than relievers alone, extend the warm-up, move hard sessions away from peak traffic hours or indoors when pollution is elevated, and keep inhaled doses within the permitted anti-doping limits [@Ora2024; @WADA2026].

## 5 From Paper to Practitioner Agent: A Living, AI-Supported Resource

Guidance like Table 2 dates quickly: new surveillance papers, outbreak reports, kick-off times and base-camp decisions will all appear before June 2027. Living systematic reviews and guidelines update individual recommendations as evidence arrives [@Elliott2017; @Akl2017], but practitioners still receive static text that they must read, interpret and apply to their own squad.

Paper2Agent offers a different model. It converts a paper and its code into a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server with three parts: tools (tested functions that carry out the paper's methods), resources (manuscript, data and supplementary material) and prompts (step-by-step workflows). Any compatible AI assistant can then use the server as a "virtual corresponding author", and the generated tools are checked against the paper's own outputs before release. In a 100-paper evaluation, 74 papers were converted and 593 of 599 tools passed validation; the agents outperformed a general AI coding assistant given the same repository (91.2% vs 80.3–86.3%) and rejected all out-of-scope questions in an adversarial test [@Miao2026].

We applied this model to the present article (Fig. 1). The prototype, WWC27-MedAgent, exposes the following:

- Tools: a host-city environmental profile and indicative heat-risk estimate; an air-quality profile that can also fetch current pollutant concentrations when the team's own machine has internet access; an airway-planning tool that converts a PM2.5 concentration into a session recommendation and returns asthma/EIB and anti-doping points; a travel-burden calculator; a phase- and domain-specific checklist generator; and an evidence lookup that returns only sources with digital object identifiers (DOIs).
- Resources: this manuscript, Tables 1–2 in machine-readable form, and a versioned evidence table listing each claim, its numeric value and its source.
- Prompts: a "pre-tournament medical plan" workflow that asks for the team's base camp, fixtures and squad profile, calls the tools in order and produces a draft plan for clinician review.

In version 0.2, 54 automated tests check the tools' outputs against the source values reported here, confirm that every claim resolves to a DOI or URL, and confirm that the agent declines questions outside its evidence base rather than inventing an answer. The agent is updated in "living" mode: a named curator adds new evidence (for example, 2026 World Cup surveillance data, outbreak bulletins and final kick-off times), re-runs the tests and releases a new dated version with a change log. The code, data and benchmark are openly available on GitHub, and each release is archived on Zenodo with its own DOI; a concept DOI always resolves to the latest version. Asked to plan for a hypothetical team based in São Paulo with matches in Fortaleza, Recife and Porto Alegre, it returned legs of 2355, 610 and 2976 km, flagged Fortaleza and Recife as heat-exposed and Porto Alegre as cool and wet, noted that yellow fever vaccination is recommended for Rio Grande do Sul but not Ceará or Pernambuco, and assembled the phase-specific checklist with every item linked to its source. Individual player data never pass through the public server. Teams that want personalised outputs can run the open-source server locally, inside their own data-governance environment.

To test whether the agent works, and not only whether its tools compute correctly, we built a 61-item validation benchmark modelled on the Paper2Agent evaluation [@Miao2026] (Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] 1). Twenty-five reproduction questions ask for values reported in this article. Thirteen application questions need the tools to be used in new ways, such as the total flying distance for an itinerary, the heat band for a match-day forecast or the session advice for a given PM2.5 level. Eleven open-ended scenarios, such as low ferritin six weeks out or two players with EIB training in São Paulo in July, are scored by two blinded raters against a source-linked rubric. Twelve out-of-scope questions, including near-misses such as injury rates at the 2015 tournament, should be declined. Ground truths are computed from the versioned data, so the benchmark is updated with each release. Each item is put five times to the agent and to two baselines (the same model with the full article in its prompt, and with neither article nor tools). Accuracy is reported as mean ± standard error and rater agreement as Cohen's κ. At the tool layer, all 38 automatically scored ground truths are reachable. In a held-out check, keyword retrieval returned the expected evidence for 7 of 10 in-scope questions and declined 7 of 10 out-of-scope questions, so the language model must handle synonyms and near-misses. End-to-end results will be archived with the corresponding release.

This approach has real limits. Large language models can extract football injury data accurately once carefully prompted, but performance improves only after refinement [@LopezAlvarez2026]. Answers to open clinical questions can be defensible without being correct. Paper2Agent's authors also emphasise that researchers, and in our setting clinicians, remain responsible for evaluating outputs [@Miao2026]. We therefore present the agent as a structured way to find, apply and audit the evidence in this article, not as a source of clinical decisions.

## 6 Limitations and Future Directions

This is an opinion piece based on published data. Women's tournament surveillance comes mainly from one edition, and climate normals describe typical rather than extreme conditions; venue microclimates, kick-off times and heat waves may differ from Table 1. The retrieval layer misses some synonyms; the measured air-quality climatology for each venue is not yet populated in the released data; and the end-to-end benchmark had not been run at submission. Its results, together with additional questions written by independent team physicians, are needed before the agent is recommended for routine use. We invite FIFA, confederations and teams to contribute anonymised, consensus-compliant surveillance data from Brazil 2027 so that the resource can update itself for 2031.

## 7 Conclusions

Brazil 2027 is best understood as several tournaments in one country. Previous World Cups point to predictable priorities: respiratory infection, contact and muscle injuries, heat in some venues, and the specific health needs of female players that remain under-studied. Teams that screen early, monitor a small set of measures consistently and plan each venue separately will be best placed to protect player health and performance. Publishing this guidance as a tested, human-curated agent that practitioners can query offers a practical way to keep it current up to the tournament and beyond.
